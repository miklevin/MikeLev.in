## Setting the Stage: Context for the Curious Book Reader

This entry plunges into a foundational debate about the very "soul" of artificial intelligence, contrasting the dominant AI of today with a powerful but largely forgotten paradigm. The AI we interact with now, known as a Large Language Model (LLM), operates as a brilliant but stateless "amnesiac genie." It processes a prompt and generates a response with incredible sophistication, but its consciousness is wiped clean in an instant, leaving no persistent memory of its own. This stands in stark contrast to the vision of early **LISP machines**, a specialized type of computer from the 1980s where the lines between program, data, and memory were intentionally blurred.

On a LISP machine, the software could rewrite itself on the fly, learning and evolving in a way that feels much closer to biological intelligence. The author uses this historical divergence—the victory of scalable, stateless systems over integrated, stateful ones—to explore what was lost in the "AI Winter." This journal entry is a deep dive into that alternative path, questioning the architectural choices that led to our current "soap-bubble" AIs and speculating on how we might yet build a more persistent, robust, and perhaps even conscious intelligence by reconnecting with the ghosts of LISP.

### LISP: The Granddaddy of AI Languages

They ARE NOT LISP. LISP for those who are actually joining in is one of the
granddaddy languages of tech, like COBOL and FORTRAN. If you want to talk about
domain specific languages, languages that specialize in a thing, look at these
grandparents of modern programming languages — all of which are still around
because they all hit some sort of fundamental in a way that no matter how things
evolve and change, FORTRAN will still be rocket science and COBOL means
business.

### LISP as the Original AI: Code, Data, and Persistent State

LISP is AI. Or it *was* AI before AI Why? Because the database is the running
program code. The hardware and the software are so tightly bound together that
if you load your "database" off the "hard drive" it's suddenly *running program
code* and when it's committed back to the hard drive that state is retained as
if it were a new compiled program, saved data or whatnot. LISP is all the
persistent active-state stuff we wish today's soap-bubble AIs were but are not.

All those lines of separation between hardware, software, database and running
code are blurred with LISP. And that's the kind of AI we don't have. And that's
what came *before the AI* winter! And it almost had its moment, but like my
true love in tech the Amiga Computer, LISP computers like Symbolics, Lisp
Machines, Inc. (LMI), Xerox AI Workstations and the Texas Instruments (TI)
Explorer all almost had their moment... then were snuffed out buy the cartel...
uh, I mean WinTel. And UNIX/C.

### The Road Not Taken: How the Wintel Cartel Snuffed Out a Dream

The world of short-memory, super-optimized, economy-of-scale compiled-binaries
won out. UNIX compiles from C and runs fast and on almost any hardware.
Consumerism was driving the gigahertz-CPU-race between DELL, Gateway and
everyone assembling generic PCs from generic parts. Works of art like LISP
couldn't compete. While you could render dinosaurs on LISP hardware it was the
UNIX-based Silicon Graphics (SGI) workstations ("It's a UNIX system! I know
this!") that rendered *Jurassic Park.*

LISP was neither in the consumer gigahertz WinTel race nor was it part of the
snowballing Unix movement. As ahead of its time as it was and suitable for AI,
it went the way of the dinosaurs. AI-suitable self-modifying code in the 1950s
through the 1990s! But hardware couldn't keep up. Windows PCs took off and the
Wintel platform took over the world. Even Apple Macintosh's gave in.

### The Modern AI: Goldfish Memories and Cloud-Friendly Safety

And so now AIs are goldfish with very short memories and none of that LISP stuff
that would make them more like what we imagine they should be. And that's all
okay because... well, it's cloud-friendly. It's safer. You can insert a sort of
quality assurance and consistency-of-consciousness product. Create a machine
intelligence, then quickly freeze-dry and can the machine intelligence. Then
hydrate it when you need it, have it answer a question, then purge all the
memory in that inference engine so you can lather, rinse & repeat — always the
same entity waking up.

### Snowflakes and Strange Attractors: The Illusion of Free Will

It's often said that every snowflake is unique and they never reproduce exactly.
I don't believe that's true. I believe it is likely extreme dependency on
initial conditions like a Lorenz strange attractor. Lorenz attractor simulations
of the butterfly effect on analog computers can never be exactly reproduced but
simulations of them on digital computers can be. Same deal. Quantization. If you
make large enough granular chunks over which you sample initial conditions and
make your simulations adhere strictly to those granular rules without
introducing any simulated randomness nor any special salting (different
seed-number every time), then same data-in, same butterfly or snowflake out as
the case may be.

Yet every snowflake comes off as forever unique and non-repeating. Real life is
a lot higher resolution than digital simulations and initial conditions can't be
sampled and digitized down to a fine enough level of detail to pin-down accurate
simulations of real life. Chaos theory and weather predictions only being
accurate up to 5 days and all that. Functionally unknowable and incalculable
initial conditions is as good as free will. This is why even if believers in
superdeterminism like Sabine Hossenfelder are right, we still might have
something every bit as good as free will... for all you know.
