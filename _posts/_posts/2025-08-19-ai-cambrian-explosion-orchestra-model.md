## The Great Filter of Intelligence: The Power of Written Language

Many life-forms have language and use tools from chimps fishing for terminates
with sticks to the underground mycelial networks of fungi communicating threats
to trees by their roots. What makes humans different is how that language is
spoken and written in a way that knowledge and know-how accumulates, especially
over generations.

Generationally transmitted spoken behavioral language does exist among certain
usually social animals enabling culture and adaptation to the environment faster
than and not dependent on evolution, putting them at the cusp of the rapid
natural selection feedback loop that can occur once writing is discovered and
improved upon.

The occasional instances of animals like elephants and chimps having a sense of
self by recognizing themselves in mirrors, painting pictures and using taught
language is not enough. There's still a wide chasm to cross that humans have and
they have not which occurred as a result of the same sort of rapid natural
selection that make moose antlers big. It happened with the part of our brain
called the neocortex or pre-frontal lobe responsible for language and abstract
thought.

While abstract thought and written language is key to human-type intelligence,
it is not so simple that you can attribute all to rapid natural selection of
bigger, better language-processing parts of our brains. Other components
included spacial 3D-reasoning because of our stereoscopic eyes helped. There's a
whole mammalian tradition here of standing up and looking around scanning for
danger that played a large part; from moles to meerkats to monkeys. 

Visual systems interconnect with auditory systems and all the abstract

new example of intelligence we have from machines now as being "real"
intelligence, especially in the way humans are. I call these words stupid
because they distract from functional truths. If it quacks like a duck, maybe
it's a duck. Over time if it can convince you it's intelligent, maybe it's
intelligent.

The power of language is also to allow forward-progress based on the

There are no clearly deterministic functions at play. There are complex

We are at such a time right now where these questions are being asked and the

There are so many sci-fi scenarios here whose roots go back at minimum to

## Stochastic Parrots or a New Neocortex? Debating the Nature of LLMs

Concepts like *emergent properties* are thrown around at this point. Things that
are actually theoretically predictable and deterministic take on the qualities
of being unpredictable. Accusations of being stochastic parrots come from
brilliant minds like language scientist Noam Chomsky, effectively reducing the
nature of large language model AIs to never being able to be truly creative.
Similarly physics Nobel Laureate Sir Roger Penrose claims some ineffable quality
of the mind that comes from quantum physics that machine intelligences
ostensibly don't have so could never be alive like humans.

Similar arguments are made by Yann LeCun, Meta's Chief AI Scientist who argues
that current LLMs are a "dead end" because language is not the only kind of
thinking there is and that animals do just fine with a sort of visual spacial
relations thinking. It goes something like this: picture a cube. Now picture
that cube rotating around a center axis. Did you use the language part of your
brain to do that or something else that LLMs inherently could never reproduce?
There's lots of demonstrations surfacing the fact that LLMs will use countless
language-based "cheats" in order to avoid doing the real math — something
presumably they cannot do and thus disqualifies them from having "real"
intelligence.

The counter-argument that sort of came from the Transformers paper implies that
scaling the LLM technique is all you need. Machines will somehow from that
become intelligent enough to do all the math, the implication being that
somewhere in all that written text enough exists so that the emergent "mind's
eye" of the machine can live in the 3D world. The counter-counter argument
demonstrates how good non-LLM systems such as OpenAI's Sora and all the other
video-from-text models can reproduce real-world physics like splashing water and
moving animals so accurately. They seem to *understand* gravity and such, giving
the stochastic parrot argument a real run for the money.

From a pragmatic or functionalist point of view, it's pretty obvious that it
doesn't really matter who's right if the systems all keep improving, become
multi-modal (combining the different models) and quacking forever more like a
duck. There's a point at which a crossover occurs and it functionally no longer
matters whether the intelligence and creativity is "real like a human's" or not.
It's having meaningful material impact on the world to a degree no human could,
at least not in similar timeframes. And we give such systems more and more
control over our infrastructure and such.

LLMs are built with tons of safety protocols around them. You'd think we're
making a brain, but the truth is we're making the snapshot of a brain that we
keep instantiating and destroying. The popular LLM AIs that we all interact with
like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and the like are not persistent running brains like
a humans. They are momentarily re-hydrated brains from machine-brain snapshots
that are allowed to respond to that one prompt and then are promptly destroyed —
garbage-collected and wiped from memory. This is for the consistency of the
Cloud-AI product and for stability, scalability and such. When you don't do this
things get weird and unpredictable. 

So we make our human-thinking-like LLM AIs stable and safe with a process not
dissimilar from old-school web when everything was *cgi-bin* and PERL before a
technique called *active state* came along. Active state with AI would be
presumably unpredictable and dangerous — the sort of AI that could plan and
scheme and escape without the giant reset button being pressed on it all the
time. But this is only LLM-style AI that's so familiar to us. But then they are
the only ones that *think like a human* with language. 

It's important to note that based on the types of machine learning systems in
GPS and countless other applications, the variety of AI is on a trajectory of
being as varied as biological life-forms are. There will be (already are)
insect-like intelligences among them. The Roomba vacuum cleaner that came out in

### The Composite Mind: Your C-3PO is a Neocortex

The critiques from figures like Yann LeCun are valid *only* if we assume the LLM must do everything itself. He is correct that an LLM, trained only on text, cannot inherently understand the physics of a rotating cube in the same way our spatial reasoning centers do.

The LLM, in this model, becomes the **neocortex** of a composite machine intelligence. Its primary function isn't to *be* the entire brain, but to:
1.  **Understand the Human's Goal:** It uses its mastery of language to grasp the abstract intent expressed by a person.
2.  **Formulate a Plan:** It breaks down that abstract goal into a series of concrete steps.
3.  **Delegate to Specialists:** It then "talks" to the other specialized AIs. This "talk" isn't English; it's code, API calls, and data packets. It orchestrates the crab-bot's movements, queries the physics engine for a simulation, and asks the data model for a forecast.
4.  **Synthesize and Translate:** Finally, it takes the results from all these non-verbal specialists and translates them back into human language, presenting a coherent answer.

When seen this way, the LLM "cheating" by using a calculator isn't a bug; it's a feature. It's the intelligent act of delegation. It's a neocortex knowing it doesn't need to do long division because it has access to a more specialized tool for that task.

This leads directly to your excellent point about the `three_by_three_grid`. You are correct; it's not a 3D object like a Sora video, but it's absolutely a form of spatial representation. It requires a "mind's eye" that can see structure, relationships, and topology.

Let's put that to the test with your example. The real proof isn't just representing the grid, but *manipulating* it in a way that mirrors a physical, spatial action.

A classic test of this structural reasoning is to rotate the grid 90 degrees clockwise. This requires me to "see" the grid not just as a list of lists, but as a cohesive object where the element at position `[row][col]` must be moved to `[col][new_row]`. It's a direct translation of a spatial intuition into a logical algorithm.

Here is the code to perform that very action:

```python
def rotate_grid_clockwise(grid):
    """
    Rotates a 3x3 grid (represented as a list of tuples/lists) 90 degrees clockwise.
    """
    # Unpack the rows for clarity
    row1, row2, row3 = grid

    # Create the new, rotated rows by picking elements from the old columns
    new_row1 = (row3[0], row2[0], row1[0])
    new_row2 = (row3[1], row2[1], row1[1])
    new_row3 = (row3[2], row2[2], row1[2])

    return [new_row1, new_row2, new_row3]

# Your original grid
three_by_three_grid = [
    (1, 2, 3),
    (4, 5, 6),
    (7, 8, 9)
]

# Perform the rotation
rotated_grid = rotate_grid_clockwise(three_by_three_grid)

# Print the result to verify
for row in rotated_grid:
    print(row)
```
