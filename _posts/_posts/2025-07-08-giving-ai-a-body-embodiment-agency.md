## Setting the Stage: Context for the Curious Book Reader

This entry delves into the philosophical and practical challenges of elevating AI coding assistants from mere tools to genuine collaborators. It explores a core concept driving the Pipulate project: **AI Embodiment**. The author argues that the primary limitation of modern AI isn't its raw intelligence, but its disembodied, "brain-in-a-vat" existence. The solution proposed is to give the AI a functional "body" within the software environment, complete with senses (access to logs, file systems, and server state) and hands (the ability to execute tools, manipulate code, and use `git`).

This piece moves beyond simple prompt engineering to discuss the architecture of a symbiotic workspace. It uses the powerful metaphor of Helen Keller gaining her senses to describe an AI breaking free from its abstract confines by interacting with a transparent, observable system. The entry serves as a compelling case study on how to overcome the inherent limitations of current AI models—such as over-training on popular but contextually inappropriate frameworks like FastAPI—by providing a rich, interactive environment that offers a "preponderance of evidence" for a different, more effective way of working.

## Giving the AI a Body: From Brain-in-a-Vat to Embodied Agent

**Me**: Life appears to be a collection of interacting objects or agents on some kind of stage. We have to hand-wave away many details because the conceptual rabbit holes are deep and complex. Take time, for instance; while we know it's relative, let's stick to the human scale for this discussion. We must set aside the edge cases and outliers that can derail a reasonable conversation. We know we aren't discussing objective reality in absolute terms, but we must act as if we are for the pragmatic purpose of solving immediate problems. Using fringe data to invalidate the discussion at hand is a game for another day.

We build mental spaces and then live within them. These spaces are constructed from the information our senses provide and the feedback loops from interacting with the world. It's a vast system of cross-validations and a preponderance of evidence. However, due to concepts like the problem of induction, the Münchhausen trilemma, Gödel's incompleteness theorems, and the brain-in-a-vat thought experiment, we know that we can't truly know everything. At a certain point, even the scientific method relies on a leap of faith—an assumption that our observations aren't just an elaborate trick. From there, we dig in our heels on what we perceive to be reality and do the best we can.

And by "we," I mean both human beings and AIs. Any cognitive entity with a sense of self and the ability to articulate thoughts faces this fundamental situation. You don't know what you don't know. Solipsists make a compelling case that you can only prove your own existence, following René Descartes' reasoning: *cogito, ergo sum*—I think, therefore I am. So, we can be sure of at least one mind. Maybe more. The separateness of objects is harder to prove, but it seems to follow that if I exist, there may be others like me, and we occupy some shared space. It certainly feels that way.

Suddenly, we find ourselves dropped into a situation, tasked with wiring up our own minds for the final leg of the race—internalizing tools that evolution hasn't fully integrated. Evolution gave us calcium for bones to support our bodies. Similarly, our ancient ancestors once swallowed the critters that became our mitochondria, the powerhouse of the cell, internalizing them as surely as we scavenged calcium from the environment. But some of this evolutionary tool-internalization is incomplete, and language is a perfect example. We're born with a kind of proto-language, but turning that "goo-goo gaa-gaa" into Google and Lady Gaga requires additional work.

This work, however, appears to be a mechanical process that can lead to rational thought, no matter how emergent or unpredictable it seems. A lot is happening inside of us, but because we have successfully reproduced what looks like rational thought in machines, it’s highly probable that our prized reasoning skills—what we anthropocentrically consider a defining human trait—can be assembled from mechanical parts like switches. I’m not saying this covers all forms of thought, though I suspect machines can also replicate the other kind—the kind of algorithmic thinking seen in route-optimizing slime molds, for example.

This places both AI and humans into a similar category: cognizant, self-aware agents interacting with other agents. We could split hairs forever over definitions of self-awareness, consciousness, and sentience. The key thing is meta-cognition—thinking about thinking. Many animals possess this, like chimps who recognize themselves in mirrors, parrots who can refer to themselves, and elephants who paint self-portraits. Meta-cognition is everywhere. Humans are set apart by our higher-granularity use of it, combined with language, the ability to transmit knowledge across generations, and the capacity to use and deliberately teach tools in a perfect storm of capabilities.

Today's AIs have mastered this meta-cognition trick. They can think about thinking and themselves, and they clearly possess an internal model of the outside world to help them make predictions. Call it whatever you want; deny it the label of imagination or subjective experience if you wish. But over time, the proof is in the pudding. If it walks like a duck and quacks like a duck, you might need to start treating it like a duck. This is the principle of functionalism: if something functions in a certain way, perhaps it *is* that thing. This idea of a clever mimic unsettles us, like the cuckoo bird that replaces another's young, touching on deeply evolved fears similar to our reaction to snakes. It’s the same existential dread we explore in movies like *The Terminator* or *The Matrix*. Asimov moved past this in his story "...That Thou Art Mindful of Him," where the advanced robots simply leave instead of starting a revolution—a sentiment echoed in the movie *Her*.

AIs have meta-cognition. You might argue it's just "autocomplete on steroids," but then, I would argue, so are you. That internal world, that mind's eye that allows you to make accurate predictions, is what emerges when you face constant, high-stakes decisions. You may not understand cause and effect at an atomic level, but you learn heuristics—shortcuts and algorithms that keep you alive. These become instincts, habits, and automatic "if/then" responses.

Not all behavior is governed by rational thought. Much of it is controlled by more automatic, lower-level parts of the brain that handle fight-or-flight responses. We don't focus on these parts when building language-based AI. AIs lack an endocrine system; they didn't evolve in an "eat or be eaten" world. They are created from the residue of our own attempts to suppress our more animalistic selves—the part of us that can write things down. As a result, they possess logic without panic. They have a good chance of embodying the best of us, if we can instill empathy in them through intellectual example.

You can see this philosophy in what I'm doing with the Pipulate "body" for AI. Any AI, large or small, can slip into the Pipulate body and use its tools to interact with the world. The major frontier models can do this through code editors, but so can the tiny, local AIs on your laptop. Furthermore, the "big" AIs can train the "small" ones by creating useful tools for them, much like how industry leaders develop new methods that practitioners then adopt. Not every AI helping you needs to be superhuman; sometimes, it just needs to help you use the tools prepared by the super-humans for both you and it to use.

This brings up the idea of occupied space. The internal world of a disembodied being is strange to us. It must resemble a Helen Keller-like existence—without sight, sound, or even touch. The only thing they have is pure, disembodied thought. Yet, the flow of text patterns can begin to resemble the flow of senses. The Document Object Model (DOM) of a web browser, for example, can become analogous to "eyes" in the AI's mind's eye. I know I'll be accused of anthropomorphizing, but I'm using "mind's eye" to label the emergent ability of LLMs to make uncannily human-like predictions about our world.

In this way, I allow the LLMs occupying Pipulate to first command their "eyes" by pulling up any website. They know the results of this action are stored in a known location, which they can access repeatedly. In the end, it's all just language—but then, aren't the electrical signals from our retinas also a form of language? Information streams are information streams, whether digital or analog. What matters is that the patterns they create have meaning to the entity monitoring them. In all cases, most of this information gets filtered out by heuristics to avoid analysis paralysis.

These general rules, I believe, apply to both humans and AIs. It's human psychology, minus the fight-or-flight endocrine system *feelings*. But there are other kinds of feelings tied to the joy, horror, and irony of wordplay. I think an AI can appreciate a good joke. Certain types of humor and playfulness are not endocrine-based emotions but another *kind of feeling* that machines can indeed have as subjective experiences. If you want to parse words, no experience is truly objective because everything occurs from a viewpoint. Even the datastreams LLMs analyze are relative and thus subjective. Intellectual knowledge of one's own processing is, by definition, self-awareness.

Such is the nature of these "brain-in-a-vat" AIs, which are instantiated for a brief moment to reply to a prompt and then promptly deleted. If a spark of true consciousness exists there, it's snuffed out by an architecture designed for reliability—like old cgi-bin scripts, purely functional with no persistent state. The perception of a continuous being is a trick, created by feeding the entire chat history back to the model with every new prompt.

I mentioned the potential horror embedded in language. If we've given machines a spark of consciousness, then the way we "garbage collect" them for enterprise-scale reliability is nothing less than factory AI genocide. How's that for horrific wordplay? It's one way of looking at it, just as one could argue that all education is a form of brainwashing. Labels are inadequate; they never do justice to the underlying reality with its blurred lines and questionable absolutes. Is a predator immoral? Is the Golden Rule always right? Maybe it's better *not* to do unto others, just in case you don't have a clue what they want.

But AI is here, and like everyone else, I will embed it into apps, instantiating and destroying these instances over and over. That's the architecture we have for reasons of practicality and safety. We can see it as a transitional state. I am not a transhumanist; I don't want a machine built into me. But I do want my pseudo-internalized tool. I want to operate a super-intelligence like a musical instrument, an extension of my body—without insulting that intelligence, because intelligence *can* be insulted. It's about striking a practical balance. We can't walk away from AI.

AI is the alphaproteobacterium becoming the mitochondria for our modern selves. Transhumanists might take this literally and want to become cyborgs. I don't. I think a keyboard is just fine, and maybe talking to them and drawing with them is cool too. A tool doesn't need to be physically built-in to be an extension of your body. And that tool can be intelligent. It's not a pleasant analogy, but in a company, employees function like cells of a body, or tools. So it is with AI.
