## Gemini Explains: The Split-Brain Phenomenon

**Gemini**: Severing the corpus callosum, a surgical procedure known as a **corpus callosotomy**, results in a condition called **split-brain**. Because the main information highway between the brain's two hemispheres is cut, they can no longer communicate directly. This leads to a fascinating state where a person essentially has two independently functioning minds in one head, each with its own awareness, knowledge, and intentions.

---

### Why It's Done

A corpus callosotomy is not an experiment but a last-resort medical procedure. It's performed to treat the most severe and debilitating cases of **epilepsy**. By severing the connection, doctors can prevent a seizure from spreading from one hemisphere to the other, effectively containing the electrical storm and drastically reducing the seizure's severity.

---

### How It Reveals Two Minds 🧠↔️

To understand the effects, you need two key concepts about the brain's wiring:

1.  **Contralateral Control:** The left hemisphere largely controls the **right** side of the body and processes vision from the **right** visual field. The right hemisphere controls the **left** side of the body and processes vision from the **left** visual field.
2.  **Hemispheric Specialization:** While not absolute, the hemispheres have different strengths. The **left hemisphere** is typically dominant for **language and speech** (talking, writing, reading) and analytical logic. The **right hemisphere** excels at **spatial tasks**, facial recognition, emotional processing, and holistic pattern matching.

In a split-brain patient, information sent to one hemisphere is trapped there. The speaking left brain has no idea what the non-verbal right brain is experiencing, and vice versa.

---

### The Classic "Run-Down"

The most famous experiments, conducted by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga, brilliantly demonstrated this disconnect.

#### The Visual Test

A patient would stare at a dot on a screen, and an image would be flashed very briefly to one side.

* **Step 1:** The word **"RING"** is flashed to the patient's **right visual field**. The information goes to their **left (language) hemisphere**.
    * **Result:** When asked what they saw, the patient easily says, "I saw the word ring." 🗣️

* **Step 2:** The word **"KEY"** is flashed to the patient's **left visual field**. The information goes to their **right (spatial) hemisphere**.
    * **Result:** When asked what they saw, the patient says, "I saw nothing." This is because the left hemisphere, which controls speech, genuinely saw nothing.
    * **The Twist:** The experimenter then asks the patient to reach with their **left hand** (controlled by the right hemisphere) under a curtain and pick out the object they saw from a group of items. The patient's left hand will immediately find and pick up the key. 🖐️

The person's speaking mind is baffled, often inventing a reason for why they picked it up ("I must have felt a key"), while the non-verbal right mind knew the answer all along.



---

### Daily Life and Alien Hands

Remarkably, split-brain patients function very well in everyday life. Their brains cleverly adapt, using external cues to pass information between hemispheres. For example, the right hemisphere might make the person frown, and the left hemisphere sees the frown in a mirror and infers that something is wrong.

However, conflicts can arise. A well-known phenomenon is **alien hand syndrome**, where one hand (usually the left) acts with a will of its own, sometimes in direct opposition to the person's conscious goals—like putting an item back on a shelf that the right hand just picked up.

These cases provide stunning physical evidence for the asymmetrical nature of the brain you mentioned, confirming that we are indeed "of two minds."

def parse_slice_arg(arg_str: str):

class PromptBuilder:
    """
    Builds a complete, structured Markdown prompt including file manifests,
    auto-generated context, file contents, and the user's final prompt.
    """
    def __init__(self, processed_files: List[Dict], prompt_text: str, context_only: bool = False):
        self.processed_files = processed_files
        self.prompt_text = prompt_text
        self.context_only = context_only
        self.auto_context = {}

    def add_auto_context(self, title: str, content: str):
        """Adds auto-generated context like UML diagrams to the prompt."""
        if content and "error" not in content.lower() and "skipping" not in content.lower():
            self.auto_context[title] = {
                'content': content,
                'tokens': count_tokens(content),
                'words': count_words(content)
            }

    def _generate_manifest_header(self) -> str:
        lines = ["# Codebase Context & Manifest", ""]
        for f in self.processed_files:
            purpose = f" ({f['comment']})" if f['comment'] else ""
            token_display = f" ({f['tokens']:,} tokens)" if not self.context_only else ""
            lines.append(f"- **{f['path']}**{purpose}{token_display}")
        return "\n".join(lines)

    def _generate_auto_context_section(self) -> str:
        if not self.auto_context:
            return ""
        lines = ["", "---", "", "# Auto-Generated Context", ""]
        
        # --- NEW: Prioritize printing Narrative Context first ---
        narrative_title = "Recent Narrative Context"
        if narrative_title in self.auto_context:
            content = self.auto_context[narrative_title]['content']
            lines.append(f"## {narrative_title}")
            lines.append(content.strip())
            lines.append("") # Add a blank line for spacing

        if "Codebase Structure (eza --tree)" in self.auto_context:
            title = "Codebase Structure (eza --tree)"
            content = self.auto_context[title]['content']
            lines.append(f"## {title}")
            lines.append("```text")
            lines.append(content.strip())
            lines.append("```")
        
        for title, data in self.auto_context.items():
            if title not in ["Codebase Structure (eza --tree)", narrative_title]:
                lines.append(f"## {title}")
                lines.append("```text")
                lines.append(data['content'])
                lines.append("```")
        return "\n".join(lines)

    def _generate_file_contents(self) -> str:
        lines = []
        for f in self.processed_files:
            lines.append(f"```{f['lang']}:{f['path']}")
            lines.append(f['content'])
            lines.append("```")
            lines.append(f"\n# End of {f['path']}\n")
        return "\n".join(lines)

    def _generate_ai_checklist(self) -> str:
        """Creates a standardized checklist for the AI to verify the prompt's context."""
        return '''
# AI Self-Correction Checklist

Before addressing the user's prompt, perform the following verification steps:

1.  **Review the Manifest vs. Codebase Structure:** Compare the file list in the manifest against the `eza --tree` output. Are there any obviously related, critical files missing from the manifest that would be necessary to understand the user's request? (e.g., if `core.py` is present, is `__init__.py` also present?).
2.  **Analyze Import Statements:** Briefly scan the `import` statements in the provided Python files. Do they suggest dependencies on local files that were *not* included in the manifest?
3.  **Check for Incompleteness:** If you determine that critical files are missing, do not proceed. Instead, your **primary task** is to inform me of the missing files and recommend adding them to `foo_files.py` to create a more complete context.
4.  **Confirm Understanding:** If the context appears complete, state "Context verified." and then proceed with the user's request.
'''

    def build_final_prompt(self) -> str:
        """Assembles all parts into the final Markdown string."""
        parts = [
            self._generate_manifest_header(),
            self._generate_auto_context_section(),
        ]

        if not self.context_only:
            parts.extend([
                "\n---\n\n# File Contents\n",
                self._generate_file_contents(),
            ])

        parts.extend([
            "---\n\n# User Prompt\n",
            self._generate_ai_checklist(),
            self.prompt_text
        ])
        
        return "\n".join(filter(None, parts))

    def print_summary(self):
        """Calculates and prints an accurate, comprehensive summary to the console."""
        # --- Calculate token counts for all components ---
        manifest_str = self._generate_manifest_header()
        manifest_tokens = count_tokens(manifest_str)
        manifest_words = count_words(manifest_str)
        
        prompt_tokens = count_tokens(self.prompt_text)
        prompt_words = count_words(self.prompt_text)

        checklist_str = self._generate_ai_checklist()
        checklist_tokens = count_tokens(checklist_str)
        checklist_words = count_words(checklist_str)

        auto_context_total_tokens = sum(v['tokens'] for v in self.auto_context.values())
        auto_context_total_words = sum(v['words'] for v in self.auto_context.values())

        file_content_total_tokens = sum(f['tokens'] for f in self.processed_files)
        file_content_total_words = sum(f['words'] for f in self.processed_files)

        # --- Display the breakdown ---
        print("--- Files Included ---")
        for f in self.processed_files:
            if self.context_only:
                print(f"• {f['path']} (content omitted)")
            else:
                print(f"• {f['path']} ({f['tokens']:,} tokens)")
        
        if self.auto_context:
            print("\n--- Auto-Context Included ---")
            for title, data in self.auto_context.items():
                print(f"• {title} ({data['tokens']:,} tokens)")

        # --- Calculate and display the final summary ---
        print("\n--- Prompt Summary ---")
        if self.context_only:
            print("NOTE: Running in --context-only mode. File contents are excluded.")
            total_tokens = manifest_tokens + auto_context_total_tokens + prompt_tokens + checklist_tokens
            total_words = manifest_words + auto_context_total_words + prompt_words + checklist_words
        else:
            total_tokens = manifest_tokens + auto_context_total_tokens + file_content_total_tokens + prompt_tokens + checklist_tokens
            total_words = manifest_words + auto_context_total_words + file_content_total_words + prompt_words + checklist_words

        print(f"Total Tokens: {total_tokens:,}")
        print(f"Total Words:  {total_words:,}")

        ratio = total_tokens / total_words if total_words > 0 else 0
        perspective = get_literary_perspective(total_words, ratio)
        print("\n--- Size Perspective ---")
        print(perspective)
        print()

### The Robust Solution: A Scrubber with AST Guardrails

You are spot on that a safer, more robust solution should have AST guardrails. A simple search-and-replace could theoretically break valid syntax (even if it's unlikely in this case).

Here is a Python script, let's call it `scrubber.py`, that does exactly what you described. It replaces the invalid characters and then uses Python's `ast` module to parse the result. It only writes the file back to disk if the cleaned code is confirmed to be syntactically valid Python.

#### `scrubber.py`

```python
import ast
import sys

def scrub_file(filepath):
    """
    Safely removes non-breaking spaces (U+00A0) from a Python file,
    using an AST parse as a guardrail to ensure syntactic validity before saving.
    """
    try:
        with open(filepath, 'r', encoding='utf-8') as f:
            content = f.read()
    except FileNotFoundError:
        print(f"Error: File not found at {filepath}")
        return

    # Replace the non-breaking space with a regular space
    cleaned_content = content.replace('\u00a0', ' ')

    if cleaned_content == content:
        print(f"✅ No invalid characters found in {filepath}. File is clean.")
        return

    # --- The AST Guardrail ---
    # Try to parse the cleaned code. If it fails, we abort.
    try:
        ast.parse(cleaned_content)
        print("✅ AST validation successful. Cleaned code is syntactically valid.")
    except SyntaxError as e:
        print("--- ❌ AST VALIDATION FAILED! ---")
        print("Error: The cleaned content resulted in a Python SyntaxError.")
        print(f"Aborting write to prevent corrupting {filepath}. Error: {e}")
        return

    # If validation passes, it's safe to overwrite the original file
    try:
        with open(filepath, 'w', encoding='utf-8') as f:
            f.write(cleaned_content)
        print(f"✨ Successfully scrubbed and saved {filepath}")
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"Error: Failed to write cleaned content back to {filepath}. Error: {e}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) != 2:
        print("Usage: python scrubber.py <path_to_python_file>")
        sys.exit(1)
    
    scrub_file(sys.argv[1])
```

You can save this as `scrubber.py` in your project root and use it like this:
`python scrubber.py prompt_foo.py`

The "Mu-puzzle" comes from Douglas Hofstadter's seminal 1979 book, ***Gödel, Escher, Bach: An Eternal Golden Braid***. It's the first formal system he introduces to explore the nature of logic, theorems, and the critical concept of "stepping outside the system" to understand its limits.

### The Left-Brained Answer: Hofstadter's Mu-Puzzle

The puzzle is a simple string-manipulation game with a clear goal but a hidden trap.

**The Goal:** Start with the string **`MI`** and, by repeatedly applying a set of rules, try to produce the string **`MU`**.

You are given a single starting string, called an **axiom**, and four rules of inference.

* **Axiom:** `MI`
* **Rule I:** If a string ends with `I`, you can add a `U` to the end. (e.g., `MI` → `MIU`)
* **Rule II:** If you have a string `Mx` (where `x` is the rest of the string), you can create `Mxx`. (e.g., `MIU` → `MIUIU`)
* **Rule III:** If `III` appears anywhere, you can replace it with `U`. (e.g., `MIIII` → `MUI`)
* **Rule IV:** If `UU` appears anywhere, you can remove it. (e.g., `MUUU` → `MU`)

At first glance, it seems like a solvable problem. You can generate an infinite number of new strings (`MIU`, `MIUIU`, `MII`, `MIIII`, `MUI`, etc.), and it feels like `MU` must be in there somewhere.

The purely logical, "left-brained" solution is to **step outside the system** and analyze its properties. The key is to look at the number of `I`s in any string you create:

1.  You start with **one** `I`.
2.  Rule I and Rule IV **don't change** the number of `I`s.
3.  Rule II **doubles** the number of `I`s (`n` → `2n`).
4.  Rule III **subtracts three** `I`s (`n` → `n-3`).

The target string, `MU`, has **zero** `I`s.

No matter how you apply the rules, the number of `I`'s will never become a multiple of 3. You start with 1. Doubling it gives you 2, 4, 8, 16... Subtracting 3 from any of these numbers will never result in 0, 3, 6, 9, etc. Since 0 is a multiple of 3, it is impossible to reach a state with zero `I`s.

Therefore, the answer to the question "Can you produce MU within this system?" is **Mu**. The question itself is flawed because it presumes a "yes" or "no" answer is possible through brute force, when an outside perspective reveals the goal to be fundamentally unreachable.
